Dengue

DENV-1 to DENV-4Aedes mosquitoViral illness

Dengue is an acute viral infection transmitted mainly by infected female Aedes mosquitoes. Most symptomatic illness is self-limited, but a subset develops plasma leakage, severe bleeding or organ dysfunction. The critical phase commonly occurs around defervescence, often around illness days 4–5.

Primary references: WHO Guidelines for Clinical Management of Arboviral Diseases (2025); Government of India, National Guidelines for Clinical Management of Dengue Fever (2023).

Etiology & transmission

  • Dengue virus, family Flaviviridae.
  • Four established serotypes: DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3 and DENV-4.
  • Primarily transmitted by infected female Aedes aegypti; Aedes albopictus can also contribute.
  • Rare alternative transmission routes include maternal transmission and transmission through blood products/organs.

Pathogenesis

  1. Viral replication and innate/adaptive immune activation.
  2. In susceptible patients, endothelial dysfunction and increased vascular permeability develop.
  3. Plasma leakage can cause hemoconcentration, pleural effusion/ascites and intravascular volume depletion.
  4. Severe disease may involve shock, severe bleeding or severe organ involvement.

Clinical phases

  • Febrile phase: high fever with headache, myalgia/arthralgia, nausea and sometimes rash.
  • Critical phase: around defervescence; plasma leakage and deterioration may occur.
  • Recovery phase: reabsorption of extravascular fluid and clinical improvement in most patients.

Clinical features

  • Acute high-grade fever
  • Headache and retro-orbital pain
  • Myalgia/arthralgia
  • Nausea/vomiting
  • Rash
  • Weakness/fatigue
  • Bleeding manifestations may occur

⚠️ Warning signs / danger features

  • Severe abdominal pain or tenderness
  • Persistent vomiting
  • Clinical fluid accumulation
  • Mucosal bleeding
  • Lethargy/restlessness or altered mental status
  • Liver enlargement
  • Progressive rise in hematocrit with falling platelet count, particularly with clinical deterioration
  • Difficulty breathing
  • Cold, pale or clammy skin / features of shock
  • Major bleeding or organ dysfunction

Important: deterioration can occur around or after fever settles. Clinical status and serial assessment matter; platelet count alone should not determine severity.

Examination

  • Vitals, perfusion and mental status
  • Hydration status and urine output
  • Bleeding: skin, gums, GI tract and other sites
  • Abdominal tenderness/hepatomegaly
  • Signs of pleural effusion or ascites
  • Signs of shock or respiratory compromise

Classification for bedside use

For practical management, assess whether the patient has dengue without warning signs, dengue with warning signs, or severe dengue. The 2023 Indian guideline additionally groups patients by clinical severity and need for outpatient observation/admission/emergency referral.

Investigations

InvestigationUse / interpretation
CBC + platelet countAssess leukopenia, thrombocytopenia and trends. Interpret with the clinical picture.
HematocritSerial change can help detect plasma leakage/hemoconcentration, especially when correlated with clinical status.
NS1 antigen / molecular testsUseful for direct detection early in illness; exact testing strategy depends on illness day and assay.
Dengue IgM / IgGUseful according to illness stage and prior exposure; interpret alongside clinical and epidemiological context.
LFT, renal function, electrolytesAssess organ involvement and guide management in patients with concerning features.
Ultrasound / chest imagingMay help detect fluid accumulation when clinically indicated.

WHO's 2025 laboratory guidance emphasizes that test choice should be guided by illness stage, specimen, local epidemiology and assay characteristics.

Management — core principles

  1. Early assessment: identify warning signs, comorbidities and ability to maintain oral intake.
  2. Hydration: encourage adequate oral fluids when appropriate. Avoid both dehydration and unnecessary over-hydration.
  3. Monitoring: clinical status, vitals, urine output, CBC/hematocrit and organ function according to severity.
  4. Warning signs/severe disease: hospital-based management with careful fluid therapy and monitoring is required.
  5. Shock: prompt assessment and appropriately titrated IV fluid resuscitation, with frequent reassessment to avoid fluid overload.
  6. Bleeding: assess severity and investigate/manage significant bleeding; blood products may be required when clinically indicated.

💊 Drugs & doses — safe reference

DrugTypical reference doseKey point
Paracetamol (acetaminophen)Adults >50 kg: 500 mg–1 g every 4–6 h; max 4 g/day. Children: 10–15 mg/kg every 4–6 h; max 60 mg/kg/day.Use the lowest appropriate dose; account for all combination products and hepatic risk. Dose references: WHO 2025 arboviral guideline and Government of India Dengue Guideline 2023. Patient-specific factors, local protocols and the latest formulary must be checked before prescribing.

Complications

  • Dengue shock / circulatory collapse
  • Severe bleeding
  • Fluid accumulation and respiratory compromise
  • Hepatic injury
  • Neurologic involvement
  • Cardiac involvement
  • Acute kidney injury and other organ dysfunction

Prevention

  • Prevent mosquito bites, especially during daytime.
  • Remove/cover water-holding containers and reduce breeding sites.
  • Use repellents, screens and protective clothing.
  • Community vector control is central to prevention.

🚨 When urgent referral is needed

Any patient with warning signs, shock/poor perfusion, respiratory distress, significant bleeding, altered consciousness, severe organ dysfunction, inability to maintain oral intake, or important high-risk comorbidity needs urgent clinical assessment and escalation according to local protocol.

📌 MBBS / exam pearls

  • Critical phase is commonly around defervescence, not necessarily during the highest fever.
  • Plasma leakage is the key pathophysiologic feature of severe dengue with shock.
  • Rising hematocrit with clinical deterioration suggests plasma leakage; a falling hematocrit in a deteriorating patient may raise concern for bleeding.
  • Do not use platelet count alone to decide whether a patient is severe.
  • Avoid aspirin and NSAIDs because of bleeding risk.
  • There is no specific antiviral treatment for dengue.
